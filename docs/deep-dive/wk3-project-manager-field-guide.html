Overview · Mission

Why the Project Manager exists

A design tells you what to build. It says nothing about the order to build it in, who owns each piece, what happens when the vendor slips, or how you will know in week three that go-live is already at risk. The Project Manager is the discipline that turns the design into a sequenced, resourced, dated plan and the cadence that keeps it honest. Skip it and even a perfect design ships late, over budget, and by heroics.

Business value
  • Makes the delivery date defensible instead of hopeful.
  • Exposes the critical path so the right work gets protected.
  • Turns risk from a surprise into a managed, owned list.
  • Gives the sponsor one honest picture of health, early.
Common misconceptions
  • "PM is updating a spreadsheet." It's sequencing work and protecting the path.
  • "A date is a plan." A date without a WBS and dependencies is a wish.
  • "Agile means no plan." It means a different planning cadence, not none.
  • "Green status is good news." Green that never moves is the most dangerous color.
How AI changes PM. AI can draft a WBS, propose a RACI, and generate a risk list in seconds. What it can't do is know which dependency is truly the long pole, which estimate is fantasy, or when to escalate. You supply judgment and the honest call; AI supplies the first draft.

Where it fits

Role in the SDLC

The Project Manager owns the Plan & Deliver spine — it picks up an approved design and runs the work all the way to a shipped increment. It doesn't decide what to build (Business Analysis) or how it's shaped (Architecture); it decides the order, the owners, the cadence, and the gates.

Section · Good vs Bad

Good vs bad project plans

Good project plans

  • State which triangle constraint is fixed (here, the go-live date).
  • Name the critical path and protect it explicitly.
  • Have milestones with real go / no-go gate criteria.
  • Carry estimates with buffer, not single-point fantasy.
  • Keep a live RAID log — every item owned and dated.
  • Report status that reflects reality, red when it's red.

Bad project plans

  • Pick the date first, then "make the work fit."
  • Show every task "on track" until the week it slips (watermelon status).
  • Map no dependencies — so everything looks parallel.
  • Use single-point estimates with no contingency.
  • Absorb scope creep silently instead of via change control.
  • Keep a RAID log that was last touched at kickoff.
Red flags in AI-drafted plans: a schedule with no critical path, every task the same tidy 2-day estimate, a RAID log of generic risks with no owners, a RACI with two "A"s on a row, and a status that stays green with no movement.
Section · Measurement

KPIs & success metrics

Delivery signals

  • Schedule variance / SPI vs baseline
  • Cost variance / CPI vs budget
  • Milestone hit rate
  • Velocity trend across sprints

Health signals

  • Blocker age — how long issues sit unresolved
  • RAID items overdue or unowned
  • % of tasks with an owner and a date
  • Burndown slope vs the ideal line
Leading vs lagging. Blocker age and burndown slope are leading — they warn you before a milestone slips. Milestone hit rate and cost variance are lagging — they confirm where the plan already broke.

Architect Review

Before you approve the plan

Run every AI-generated plan through this lens before you baseline it.

Scope & sequence

  • Does the WBS cover 100% of scope, no overlap?
  • Is the critical path identified?
  • Are dependencies explicit, not assumed?

Estimates & risk

  • Do estimates carry buffer, not single points?
  • Are the top risks owned with mitigations?
  • Which triangle constraint is fixed?

Ownership

  • Exactly one Accountable per RACI row?
  • Does every task have an owner and a date?
  • Who signs off each gate?

Cadence & control

  • Is the method (agile / hybrid) fit for the risk?
  • Are milestones gated with exit criteria?
  • How will status stay honest?
Approve only when: the WBS is complete, the critical path is named and protected, estimates carry buffer, every RAID and RACI row has one clear owner, gates have exit criteria, and the fixed constraint is stated.
Inspect Your Build

What to check in your Field Guide

After Claude Code builds your Field Guide, open it and check these — this is how you practice the architect's review:

  • Charter — does it state the sponsor, the fixed constraint, and measurable success criteria?
  • WBS — does it add up to the whole scope with no overlapping work packages?
  • Gantt — is the critical path visibly marked, and are dependencies drawn, not implied?
  • RAID log — does every item have an owner and a due date, or are they generic placeholders?
  • RACI — is there exactly one Accountable per row? Any row with two "A"s is broken.
  • Burndown / burn — do the charts show a trend against an ideal line, not a lone bar?
  • Ask assistant — try asking it "what is on the critical path?" and see if it answers from your docs.

Knowledge Base

Glossary & FAQ

Iron triangle

Scope, schedule, and cost are linked — change one and at least one other must move.

WBS

Work Breakdown Structure — the whole scope decomposed into work packages that sum to 100%.

Critical path

The longest chain of dependent tasks; any slip on it slips the whole project.

Float / slack

How long a task can slip before it delays the project. Critical-path tasks have zero.

Milestone & gate

A checkpoint with explicit go / no-go exit criteria that must be met to proceed.

RAID

Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies — the log that keeps surprises owned.

RACI

Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed — one Accountable per activity.

Baseline

The approved plan you measure variance against; changes go through change control.

Velocity

Work a team completes per sprint, used to forecast — a trend, not one number.

Burndown

Work remaining over time vs an ideal line; the earliest honest schedule signal.

Change control

The disciplined path for altering baselined scope, schedule, or cost.

RAG status

Red / Amber / Green health against baseline; green that never moves is a warning.

1. Why find the critical path before committing a date?
Because the critical path is the longest chain of dependent work — it sets the earliest possible finish. A date that ignores it is a wish, not a plan.
2. What's wrong with a RACI row that has two "A"s?
Two Accountables means no one is accountable. Exactly one person must own the outcome of each activity, or decisions stall.
3. When is hybrid better than pure agile or waterfall?
When part of the work is exploratory (build the portal UI in sprints) and part is gate-bound (compliance and vendor integration need fixed sign-offs). Hybrid runs both cadences at once.
