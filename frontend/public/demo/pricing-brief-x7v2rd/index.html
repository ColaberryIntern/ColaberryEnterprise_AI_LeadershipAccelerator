2What we are building

The very next thing is December, not February. That part matters, so it is first.

December is the proof, February is the product

In December we run their real December billing through our system alongside their spreadsheet and check that every number matches, on the same 65 requisitions. Nobody stops using Excel. It is a side-by-side test, and it happens during their normal 14th-to-16th window.

That does two things: it proves the math on their own live month, and it is where the savings stop being my estimate and become a measured number. Everything on the chart in the next section gets replaced by what we actually observe that week.

February is when they stop using the spreadsheet, because the approvals, reminders and signatures are built by then. December proves it. February is when they live on it.

4How I would think about billing it

Again, my thinking only. You set the number.

I would do a one-time fee to get them live, then a monthly retainer. Not one or the other. A single up-front fee prices it like a custom build and the relationship ends at go-live, which is exactly when the useful information starts showing up. A retainer with no up-front asks them to fund our build while they wait.

StageWhat is liveOne-timePer month
Go liveThe billing run, end to end $12,000$1,500
+ EstimatingJobs start themselves included$1,750

That is $24,000 a year once everything is live, and about $21,000 in the first year while it ramps.

Why the retainer and not just a bigger up-front: the savings grow. Month one is the worst month this product will ever have. Every month they use it we find another piece of their process worth automating, and the retainer is what pays for that to keep happening. It also means everything on the “later” list arrives without a new negotiation each time, which is an easy thing to sell.

How I justify the number

Four ways of getting there. They are independent of each other, and they land in roughly the same place, which is the part that makes it hold up.

The angleThe arithmeticLands at
1. Against what the problem costs them Their billing run is 21 person-days a month. At $55 an hour that is $110,880 a year in people’s time, before any rebuild. We are asking for 22% of that to take most of it away. $24,000
2. Against Textura’s published rate Oracle charges subcontractors 0.22% of contract value. Our $24,000 is what that rate produces on about $11 million of contract value. At $8M Textura would be $17,600; at $12M it would be $26,400. If Middlesex bill anywhere near $9 to $12 million, this is their own industry’s pricing model landing on our number. $17.6k–$26.4k
3. Against the monthly saving Once mature the product takes $6,600 a month off their run. The fee is $2,000. They keep roughly three dollars for every one they spend, every month, and it pays for itself in month 7. 3.3x monthly
4. Against what it costs us Roughly 10 engineer-weeks to get them live. The $12,000 up-front does not cover that, deliberately. We are investing in a product we keep and a reference in an industry where we want nine more clients. The retainer is how that comes back. below cost
Why this number is comfortable rather than tight

At $55 an hour of their time it pays for itself in month 7 and returns 2.21x over two years. Even at $40 an hour, which is about as low as their loaded cost could plausibly be, it still breaks even in month 11 and returns 1.61x.

That is the test I care about: it works even if we are wrong about the savings. There is no rate on that slider above $40 where this becomes a bad deal for them, which means you are not selling against the arithmetic.

One more thing worth knowing: they have already paid us $10,000, which bought the discovery and the prototype they just saw. I think there is a founding-customer gesture available there if you need one, but I would trade it for something: a reference, a case study, or letting us reuse their GC paperwork as the template for the next glazing company.

The honest tension, so it does not ambush you

Knowify publishes at $329 a month and I am suggesting $2,000. If Nick googles this, that is the first thing he will say, and “ours is better” is not an answer that survives it.

The real answer is that those are licenses and this is a build. Knowify and Siteline sell a product and you change how you work to fit it. We are building Middlesex’s actual process, including the parts your own research found nobody handles well, and the monthly fee keeps that development going rather than just keeping a login open.

It helps to split the monthly when you present it: call roughly $400 of it the platform, which is right in Knowify and Siteline territory, and the other $1,600 continuing development. Two things, not one inflated one. Nobody argues that a developer costs $1,600 a month.

The comparison I would actually use

Textura charges subcontractors 0.22% of contract value. That is the fairest benchmark on the list, because it scales with the business rather than with seats, and it is a real published rate from the biggest name in the category.

So the question for Nick is simple: what do you bill in a year? Our $24,000 is exactly what Textura’s own rate produces on about $11 million of contract value. Get that number and the pricing conversation stops being about our opinion and starts being about a rate Oracle already publishes.

One more useful thing from your page: the GC portals are downstream of us, not competing with us. Whichever portal a GC mandates, Middlesex still needs an internal place to build and approve the package before it gets filed. That is the product, and your research says the same.

6Two things I would not promise

1. Do not promise remote notarization

Your own research caught this and it is a good catch. It is also sharper than I realized: Massachusetts passed remote online notarization, but the Secretary of the Commonwealth has not released the required training or the notification form, and notaries are currently told not to use online platforms at all.

So notarizing stays an option only where a GC or another state’s form actually calls for it. If we promise MA remote notarization and the Commonwealth has simply not turned it on, that is the kind of thing that costs us the account.

There is a second piece in your page I would not have known, and it is a real build requirement rather than a caveat: the Massachusetts statutory waiver form under c.254 is unusual, has no notary block, and ordinary subcontractors often cannot use it the way out-of-state GCs expect. So we support each GC’s own required form and flag the Massachusetts limits, rather than inventing a Massachusetts form that does not legally exist. That went straight into the build list.

2. Do not put a firm annual number on it this week

The real savings figure arrives around 19 December, after they run their December billing both ways and we measure it. Before that, anything we quote is our estimate.

I would open with the shape of the deal and a budget range now, then come back in late December and set the monthly number against what we actually measured. If Nick pushes for a number this week, the range in the chart is the honest answer, and it is his own numbers driving it.
