Software Factory

Describe the workflow. See the solution. Approve it before it is built.

Most software disappointment is decided before anyone writes code, in the gap between what a client described and what a team heard.

From a described problem to an operated application

  1. Guided discovery

    You describe the workflow that costs you time. The output is written requirements you can read and correct, not notes from a call.

  2. More than one direction

    Interface directions are produced to compare, not a single take to accept or reject. Comparing two honest options surfaces what you actually wanted.

  3. Your decision, recorded

    Compare, comment, combine, request changes. Acceptance is an explicit act and it is recorded as one.

  4. Construction

    Building starts against an approved design rather than a moving target.

  5. Quality evidence

    Checks run before release, and the result is visible to you rather than asserted in a status meeting.

  6. Change, handled as change

    Later alterations are recorded as change requests with their impact, instead of quietly absorbed and quietly late.

  7. Release and operate

    Releases have a history. The application keeps being someone's responsibility after launch.

Why the approval step is the product

The expensive failure in custom software is not a bug. It is building the wrong thing correctly, discovering it late, and having no record of who understood what.

Design before build

Changing a direction you are looking at costs a conversation. Changing a system that has been built costs a project.

Acceptance on the record

Recorded acceptance protects both sides. It is also what makes a later change request a normal event rather than an argument.
