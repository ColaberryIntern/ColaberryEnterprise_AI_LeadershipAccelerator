Delivery standard

What we hold ourselves to, and how you check it.

This is the page we would want to read if we were buying. Every commitment on it is enforced by the system rather than promised by a person.

Five commitments

Ready and authorised are different states
A finished build is not a release. Releases are approved by a named person and recorded with the time and the identity. There is no path that authorises a release without one.
A waived check is never shown as a passed one
Requirements can be waived when the reason is good — an internal tool behind single sign-on may not need a public accessibility audit. The waiver is recorded with its written justification and shown to you as Waived, beside the reason. The failure we engineer against is not a gate that fails to block; it is a gate that stops blocking for a reason nobody can find afterwards.
Unknown is a state, not a zero
If an outcome was never measured, your room says Unknown. A dashboard that renders green because no data arrived is the specific failure worth designing against.
Your room shows your engagement, and nothing else
Builder notes, agent working files, internal economics, credentials and engineering logs are not hidden from your screen — they are never sent to it. The server assembles a client view from a list of permitted fields, so a new internal field is invisible to you by default rather than by vigilance.
Decisions carry their reasoning
Every decision records what was asked, what was recommended, what was chosen and why. A decision with a status and no statement of what was decided is not a record; it is a receipt.

How you check any of this

You get a login. The room shows the decisions waiting on you, the designs, the previews, the change requests and their impact, the releases with their evidence, and the documents published to you. Where a section has nothing in it, it says what has to happen before it does — it is never filled with something plausible.
